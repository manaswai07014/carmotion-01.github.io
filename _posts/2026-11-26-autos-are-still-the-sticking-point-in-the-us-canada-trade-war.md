Both sets of tariffs include exemptions for locally made parts, which lowers the effective tariff rate on Canadian vehicles exported to the United States to about 12.5 per cent and U.S. Exports to Canada to about 21.25 per cent — though the exact rate varies by plant and model.

Trump has repeatedly claimed the United States does not need Canadian-made cars and pressed automakers to shift production south of the border. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney has maintained that any agreement with the United States must leave Canada with a viable auto sector.

As negotiators closed in on a deal this summer by pitching a broader parts exemption, the U.S. Offer would have left Canadian autos facing up an effective tariff of up to 7.5 per cent tariffs, he said. While an improvement over the roughly 12.5 per cent rate automakers face today, it is still higher than the industry's profit margin, making it too high for Ottawa to accept, Volpe added.

Canadian negotiators are "not religious" about what mechanism is used to bring the effective tariff rate down, Volpe said, but want to put plants on both sides of the border back on an even playing field.

Parts compliant with the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement reach U.S. Assembly lines tariff-free, but American-made vehicles compliant with the trade deal can include 25 per cent parts content from overseas. That content faces up to 25-per-cent tariffs, translating into a tariff rate on U.S.-made vehicles of as much as 6.5 per cent.

The five automakers that currently produce vehicles in Canada — the Big Three, Honda and Toyota — are pushing hard for an interim deal that would close or eliminate the tariff gap between Canadian and U.S. Plants, even if it's a short-term solution.
