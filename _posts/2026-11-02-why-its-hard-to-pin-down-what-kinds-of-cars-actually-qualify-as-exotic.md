Humans love to label and categorize everything because it helps us make sense of the world. Cars are no exception. In many people's eyes, the Porsche 911 Turbo S, for example, is a sports car, not a supercar, and the same goes for the Corvette C8 Z06. Meanwhile, the Ferrari F430 and McLaren 570S are supercars, and the McLaren F1 is a hypercar. Yet looking at the actual numbers, the 911 and the ZR1 are both quicker than the aforementioned supercar and hypercar, which makes no sense.

Lamborghini Miura is often credited as the car that popularized the term "supercar," but the word itself existed long before the Miura. So, even pinning down the first supercar is a mess, which tells you everything about how these labels work. And that's exactly why nailing down what qualifies as exotic is just as messy.

Materials and shape matter too – heavy use of exclusive materials such as carbon fiber and titanium, wrapped in a body that sits low and narrow, only add to the case. Hagerty, meanwhile, treats the term "exotic" as an insurance-industry catch-all rather than a strict definition — one wide enough to cover everything from an entry-level luxury sports car like the BMW M6 to a full-blown hypercar like the McLaren Senna.

In his YouTube video aptly titled "Exotic Cars You're Wrong About," Doug DeMuro pointed out that Acura built fewer than 3,000 units of the second-generation NSX, calling it "a smaller production run than the Ford GT, which is a car that everybody thinks is such an iconic, special, amazing vehicle." By that logic, the NSX is rarer. Yet the NSX's exotic status is up for debate, while the Ford GT's mostly isn't.

Enthusiasts are also divided. In a FerrariChat debate about the V10 Audi R8, one person argued that mass carmakers couldn't build exotics; others pointed to the Ford GT as a counterexample. A Reddit discussion even split over the Porsche 918, with one commenter excluding every Porsche and another insisting the 918 qualified. These arguments suggest the disagreement never ends. Some judge the individual car's merits; others give more weight to the badge.

Just dance/Shutterstock
To help us understand what an exotic car might be, we can also try to look at it from a different direction — by telling you what it isn't. For starters, an exotic car is clearly not your run-of-the-mill economy car like the Nissan Altima, Honda Odyssey, or RAV4. These cars are built in massive numbers, are always accessible, offer adequate performance for daily driving, and most importantly, they don't try to be exotic in any way.

One step above these are your performance versions of regular production cars. Think the BMW M3 or a Scat Pack Challenger. These do have elements of exotic cars on them, but as a whole, most people would agree that they are not exotics. Then we get to cars like the Porsche 911, the Chevy Corvette, and most interestingly, the Audi R8. The R8 features a naturally aspirated mid-engined V10 and shares both its engine and its platform with the Lamborghini Huracan. However, the Lamborghini will usually be considered as exotic because it is a Lamborghini, while the Audi is, well, an Audi. Even the most expensive exotics can share parts with cheap normie cars, but the R8 is no normie car. But once again, it can't be considered an exotic if this reputation does not stick.
