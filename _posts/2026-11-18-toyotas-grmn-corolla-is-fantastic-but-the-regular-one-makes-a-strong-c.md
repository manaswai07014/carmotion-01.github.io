Distillation is a great process. It can extract the very best of something when done right. It doesn't require throwing away the recipe and starting with new ingredients. That said, it does require knowing what's working, what isn't, and then trimming or adding in the right places. That's exactly what Toyota has done with the GR Corolla since the very first year it existed.

When it first launched, the recipe was simple. Three cylinders, six manually selected gears, and the ability to send more power to the rear tires than to the front. For the first year of production, there was a Morizo edition with a lot of carbon fiber added and the entire rear seat removed. Now, for 2027, there's a new ultimate Corolla, but it's not the GR.

Meet the GRMN Corolla, the new top of the GR Corolla range. It's the latest expression of Toyota's willingness to keep throwing engineering resources at its most entertaining grocery getter. It's sharper, more focused, and sufficiently aggressive-looking to pick up where the Subaru WRX STI hatchback left off. It also starts at $64,360 before destination.

I've been lucky enough to drive basically every publicly available version of the GR Corolla to ever exist. When the car first launched in Utah, Toyota brought each trim level, including the Morizo, and let us wail on it all day. Then, last year, we drove not just each of the latest trim levels, but also examples from the two previous model years, so we could experience the differences the distillation process brought.

From just about any distance where it's discernible, there's no chance of mistaking the GRMN Corolla for the regular one. That's not to put down the normal GR. It has great hot hatch proportions. The GRMN just builds on that with a larger rear wing, slight changes to the front fascia, and big, bulky carbon fiber front fenders. Matte-bronze forged wheels complete the look.

As a whole, it looks harmonious and aggressive, which is key because it's not all show. Toyota openly says that the aerodynamic changes here draw on its experience in Super Taikyu racing and Nürburgring testing. The rear wing offers five adjustment positions, and the hood and fender ducts contribute to cooling and aerodynamic performance.

It's worth quickly touching on the GR Corolla itself as well. It continues on with minimal changes compared to last year. The hood bulge still looks great and offers functional vents on the mid-tier Premium Plus trim. Buyers who just pony up the cash for the base car can also add a spoiler to the rear hatch for some added flair. It's a good-looking car no matter where you fall in the trim lineup.

Stephen Rivers
Welcome to the one giant and consistent complaint that haunts the GR Corolla. Regardless of price point, the cabin has never felt as special or premium as customers seem to expect. The GRMN sort of proves this point on its own. Despite costing nearly as much as a base Corvette, the materials and design don't feel wildly special.

Sure, there are sport seats with suede-like Brin Naub and faux leather. Flocked surfaces across the dash and front pillars look nice. The carbon trim throughout the cabin is real. The steering wheel, shifter, and other controls all feel great. But something still seems to be missing from a car priced at $66,000+. The Premium Plus and Base models feel even more spartan.

If you can get over the lack of specialness inside, the rest of the interior experience is quite good. At six-foot-six, I fit very comfortably, even with a helmet on. That's impressive for such a small car. I wouldn't like to sit in the back for a road trip, but that's equal parts a lack of rear-seat headroom for someone my height and a deep personal desire to drive this thing if I have the chance. Of course, that doesn't matter much in the GRMN because there isn't a back seat to speak of. That's right: four doors and two seats, just like the Morizo years ago.

From a tech perspective, the car comes with a 12.3-inch digital gauge cluster and an 8-inch infotainment system. It offers Android Auto and Apple CarPlay and is a breeze to navigate. Toyota also offers drivers the ability to select from three different drive modes, including normal, gravel, and track. These are different from when the car originally launched. Back then, drivers could select the torque split and put up to 70 percent of the power to the back tires. That option is now gone in lieu of letting the car work out how to distribute power after you tell it what surface you're on.
