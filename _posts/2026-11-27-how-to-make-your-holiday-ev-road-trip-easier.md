Holiday weeks put more drivers on the road than almost any other stretch of the year, and electric cars add a wrinkle that gas drivers never think about: where, when, and how long you stop to charge. Done right, you can cross a busy travel corridor with no waits and a lower bill. Done wrong, you land at the one crowded Supercharger halfway between two cities on the worst afternoon of the year and sit in a line. What follows is the process, in the order you should run it, to avoid that.

If you have home charging, plug in the night before so the car is ready in the morning. Tesla's usual warning against topping to 100 percent every day is meant for batteries left sitting full for weeks, and a trip departure is precisely the case that warning carves out. Charging all the way up occasionally for a trip is fine. The one rule: after the charge finishes, leave soon, especially in cold weather.

Route planning apps such as A Better Route Planner, Plugshare, and Google Maps weigh each path against your car's range, and they will suggest a detour if a slightly different route delivers a big range boost while staying nearly as quick. They map out each stop by efficiency, destination, the state of charge you want on arrival, and road conditions, and you can tune them to the efficiency you actually get or to how often you want to stop.

An increasing number of highway stops now cluster several competing stations together near high-power infrastructure. Brands you might find side by side include Tesla Supercharging, Ionna Rechargery, EV Go, Circle K, and Shell. Research which companies sit on your route, then install their apps and set up payment before you arrive. Fumbling with a new account at the plug is how a 20-minute stop becomes an hour.

The data is blunt about when to charge. Paren, which monitors public charging across every network, pegged the worst Thanksgiving window at roughly 10 a.m. To 2 p.m., with the stretch from about midnight to 6 a.m. Drawing the fewest cars. The best times to plug in are early morning before 10 a.m. Or overnight. The midday line forms for a simple reason: everyone who left at 8 a.m. Reaches that same stop two or three hours down the road.

Tesla's trip planner tends to funnel drivers to the same handful of efficient stops, usually the site around two to three hours from a major city. Pulling off 20 minutes early at a quieter location, perhaps a small town, a hotel, or a station just off the highway, usually clears the wait when the obvious one is nearly full. If a main stop shows few free stalls, pull in one stop earlier and charge to around 70%.

Keep a backup Supercharger for every stop. On a Tesla, you can check busyness ahead of time: select the Supercharger from the car's map or the app to see how many stalls are free, and the trip planner factors in live availability.
