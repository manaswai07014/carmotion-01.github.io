## The Story

Behind the Neue Klasse design, the eighth-generation 3-series gets the basics right.

BMWJump to:How Does It Drive?What’s It Like to Live With?Should I Buy One?Highlights and Lowlights:The BMW 3-series is no longer the company’s best-selling car in the United States, but it is still the model closest to the formula that earned the brand its success and status with American enthusiasts. The all-new G50 is the eighth generation of 3-series and will be reaching the U.S. Next year, with buyers initially able to choose between a four-cylinder 330 and an inline-six-powered M350 xDrive. It’s the latter, in European specification, that’s being tested here. Is this still the heart of the BMW brand?.

Another product of BMW’s Neue Klasse era, the new 3-series gets radical new styling that closely aligns it with the fully electric i3 that will be sold alongside it (which we test separately here). But don’t let the looks fool you—beneath the surface, the two cars sit on different architectures and have subtly different dimensions. The gasoline-powered version is fractionally shorter and sits on a 112.6-inch wheelbase, 1.5 inches less than the EV’s.

The new M350 effectively replaces the previous G20-generation M340i, using a version of BMW’s long-serving B58 turbocharged inline-six that works in conjunction with a 48-volt mild hybrid system. This makes 437 horsepower and 428 lb-ft of torque, increases of 55 hp and 59 lb-ft over the outgoing car.

Drive is delivered through an eight-speed automatic transmission and an xDrive-branded all-wheel system as standard. The option of rear-wheel drive is no longer available. BMW plans to offer a switchable rear-wheel drive mode, but this wasn’t on the car R&amp;T tested in France, as the company plans to introduce it later though a software update.

The good news is the new car absolutely delivers on raw performance. BMW claims a 3.9-second 0–60-mph time, one that is almost certain to be substantially bettered with the scientifically applied brutality of instrumented testing. And the car feels every bit as quick as that number suggests, launching with M-appropriate seriousness and with huge, linear urge that barely diminishes as speeds turn extra-legal. It sounds good too, with a snarling exhaust note that proved to be mostly organic even when I deactivated the switchable active noise enhancement, but which wasn’t intrusive or droning at cruising speeds.

At lower revs there is a hint of turbo lag, the hybrid system not bringing enough punch to cancel this out, but the quick-shifting gearbox means I had to hunt this down by using the transmission’s manual mode to keep revs low. But out of the box, it feels like a substantial performance upgrade over the outgoing G20-generation M340i.

All the horses and pounds of twist are delivered to the road with a minimum of drama. On my test route in the south of France, the M350 punched out of tight corners without a hint of slip, only the combination of big throttle openings with high cornering loads creating any sense that the majority of power was traveling to the rear wheels. The M350’s dynamic brain kept everything under tight control, and while the forthcoming Drift Moment mode will doubtless unlock a wilder side to the car’s character, but on dry, smooth asphalt the lateral-g loadings generated by the Bridgestone Potenza tires were high enough that I didn’t feel any urge to go and explore the limits of grip on a tight mountain road.

Of course, the presence of occasional, accessible drama has long been a core part of the appeal of punchier M-branded BMWs, and on first impressions, this definitely felt to be lacking. The different dynamic modes did change the M350’s demeanor, with Sport and Sport Plus adjusting throttle mapping and steering weight. But none turned it into a party animal. This is one of those cars that is all business. The combination of true excitement and a gasoline engine will likely require waiting for the forthcoming combustion G84 M3, which will ultimately sit alongside the EV version.

It’s been a long while since steering excellence came as standard on most BMW models. Although the M350’s slightly ovoid steering wheel delivered crisp, linear front-end responses, actual feedback was muted and only present at all once the firm hand of the active lane-keeping system was turned off. The car felt stable and impressively precise in faster corners, but at lower speeds, the assistance weighting was too soft for my tastes in all its switchable modes.

The gentler side of the M350’s personality earns unstinted praise. Ride quality has improved markedly over the M340i, with the new car’s adaptive dampers dealing with every road surface I could find to throw at them. Beyond the engine noise—which is definitely a plus—the cabin stayed impressively quiet for a sports sedan, one area where BMW had clearly transferred learning from its well-insulated EVs. And the Highway Assistant system, a hands-off Super Cruise–style enhanced cruise control, kept the car centered in its lane, reacted to other vehicles intelligently, and even executed lane changes in response to a turn signal input. I did only a few kilometers of high-speed French Autoroute, but it was enough to show the M350 will be a relaxed, high-speed cruiser.
