Emissions compliance has been a thorn in the side of the automotive industry for years. While some of that is the result of growing pains necessary to build a better car, select features have proven so unpopular with drivers that they’ll actively avoid them whenever possible.

Diesel ownership has declined immensely in the United States and Europe since 2015 due to fallout from the industry failing to adhere to stringent emission regulations (e.g. Dieselgate). While they’re still in broad use within the realm of commercial vehicles, diesel engines have become exceptionally rare on the passenger side of the market due to rising costs and additional maintenance concerns.

Regulations have effectively taken them off the table for the vast majority of drivers and entire vehicle segments have been wiped out as a result. Gasoline-powered automobiles have had it significantly easier. But they’ve similarly found themselves hampered by years of government regulations. This has resulted in headaches for millions of customers who don’t want to make compromises on major purchases for the sake of emissions mitigation. Even manufacturers have found themselves caught in situations where chasing regulatory compliance ended up creating extra problems.

Whenever the automotive industry decides to introduce a bunch of new technologies, the move is typically accompanied by a sudden decline in average reliability. This is cyclical and frequently pertains to regulatory activity. For us, noticeable changes started to manifest around 2011. But the severity of the issue and how much it matters to individual customers varies significantly.

Some buyers won’t even consider the first model year of any vehicle, regardless of how minor the updates happen to be, due to concerns regarding novel tech. They value dependability above everything else and are doing whatever they can to mitigate risk. Others will actively seek out and spend more for an automobile that’s promising to include the latest technologies in the manufacturer’s arsenal — whether that pertains to software-based features or the physical hardware.

Finding the correct balance is something manufacturers have long had to contend with. But it seems like the industry has been catering primarily to the latter group for at least a decade, which would be fine if we weren’t also living through an era where vehicles have become the target of prolonged government meddling. Regulations have forced a deluge of emissions compliance, resulting in loads of new systems ready to save you less than a tank of fuel per year (on average) in exchange for being a constant annoyance.
