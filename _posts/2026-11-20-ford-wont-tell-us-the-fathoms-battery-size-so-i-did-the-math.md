## The Story

Ford wouldn&#x27;t share the battery capacity for the $30,000 Fathom EV truck&#x27;s base pack, but its new LFP cells gave us enough clues to do the math.

We now know how large one of the $30,000 Fathom’s battery cells is and how many cells are in the pack, which gives us a good idea of how much energy it can hold.

We’re still about a year away from the Ford Fathom going on sale and the details continue to drip out at what’s frankly a painful pace for those of us who salivate at the idea of an affordable, practical, and fun-to-drive electric truck. Yesterday, I got a tour of the Marshall, Michigan, factory the just weeks ago started producing the lithium-iron phosphate (LFP) batteries that will power the entry $29,945 Fathom.

For Ford, it was a moment to proudly show off what $3 billion has bought it: the first battery plant in America to produce LFP batteries for automotive use. For me personally, it was a fun chance to geek out with a bunch of brilliant engineers. But for the average car buyer and might-be Fathom fans, the tour was entirely devoid of the juicy specs we’re all hankering for.

Or that’s what I thought at first. Then on the drive home it dawned on me that I could math my way to a reasonable estimate of the Fathom’s battery capacity. Ford engineers didn’t want to talk about any battery specs, but we did get to hold the cells several times throughout the tour and I got the idea to use my cell phone as a makeshift ruler. That’s why I can tell you that the battery cell measures approximately as wide as a Pixel 8a phone, 1.5 times as long as the phone is tall, and with a height about 1.5 times the phone’s width. Based on these extremely precise measurements, I’m estimating the cell volume at 1.75 liters.

A quick query of your favorite AI chatbot will tell you that that current state-of-the-art LFP batteries have a volumetric energy density of 340-350 watt-hours/liter. That suggests each cell stores between 595 and 613 watt-hours.

The last missing piece, which Ford engineers didn’t want to share, was how many cells are in each pack. I got that number from a cutaway display and an up-close look at the bus bar that connects all 108 cells together. From here it’s simple arithmetic. Multiply the number of cells by the volume per cell and the energy density and you’ll get 64.3 to 66.2 kWh.

Ford actually gave me a way to spot check this math, too. I was told that once Blue Oval Battery Park Michigan is up to full speed it will spit out 80 cells every minute and enough batteries in a year to store 20 gigawatt-hours of energy. With that, we just need to know how many minutes the plant will run every year.

This will again require some imperfect estimating. We know that Ford is planning to run the plant around the clock using four teams on 12-hour shifts. Of course, that’s not to say that it will be running at full capacity every minute of the year. There will be downtime, both scheduled and unscheduled. Sam Abuelsamid, an industry analyst at Telemetry, estimates that the plant would actually be productive 48 weeks a year and 20 hours per day—or 403,200 minutes a year. That would net about 32 million cells. Split the plant’s 20 total gigawatt-hours across 32 million batteries and you end up with a per-cell energy capacity of 620 watt-hours. A pack with 108 of those cells would store 67.0 kWh. That suggests my initial estimates are in the ballpark.

We’re still a good distance from what may be the most important number for customers: range. A capacity between 64 and 67 kWh would make the Fathom’s battery around the same size as the Chevrolet Bolt’s. Ford’s truck, which we’re told has more cabin space than a Toyota RAV4, will almost certainly be wider and heavier than the dainty Bolt and its aerodynamics less forgiving. Even with Ford’s ruthless focus on efficiency, I don’t expect the Fathom to beat the Bolt’s 262 miles of range. At an EPA efficiency of 3.5 miles per kWh, the Fathom would be about 12 percent less efficient than a Bolt or Tesla Model 3 and deliver around 220 to 230 miles.

Remember, though, that this all concerns the standard battery pack. We know there will be larger batteries with more range. Alan Clarke, the VP who is leading development of the Fathom and Ford’s Universal EV platform, has confirmed that the Fathom will be available with at least 300 miles of range using an optional nickel-based lithium-ion battery with more capacity.

Of course, you should read these numbers as educated estimates rather than fact at this point. Whether I’m right or wrong here is just one more thing we’ll have to wait to find out once Ford finally drops the key specs on the Fathom.
