The good news for anyone eyeing a high-mileage electric truck: pulling the battery out of a Ford F-150 Lightning is, in raw mechanical terms, simpler than yanking an internal combustion engine. The bad news is that the pack weighs roughly as much as a small car, and one wrong move with a scissor lift can leave you with a broken connector and a ruined coolant hose.

Here is what the job actually demands, based on a real swap performed after a Lightning's battery failed at 234,000 miles driven in three years — a pace that works out to a couple hundred miles every single day of ownership, assuming the truck never took a day off.

You will need a proper lift, a hydraulic device capable of supporting the pack, coolant, and the replacement battery itself. This is not a driveway job with a floor jack. What follows is the order the work must happen in, and what a clean result looks like when you are done.

An EV battery pack is heavier than almost any engine you will find in a road car. The Lightning's standard unit is a 98 kWh pack that weighs around 1,800 pounds. For comparison, the Hyundai Ioniq 5 carries a 63 kWh battery that weighs 815 pounds — less than half. That single number governs everything about the job. Without the right equipment to move that mass safely, you do not have a repair; you have a hazard suspended over your head.

Here is the encouraging part. Once you have the gear to handle the weight, the swap is considered easier than pulling an engine. An engine comes out only after you fight numerous hoses, wires, and mounts, and detach it from the transmission. A battery pack has far fewer connections to break loose. Fewer points of failure, fewer chances to forget something on reassembly.

The procedure itself is straightforward in outline. You raise the truck, drain out the coolant, disconnect the high-voltage cables, and unbolt the battery. Coolant first, because the pack is liquid-cooled and those lines run into it. The lines get disconnected here and reconnected at the end.

The battery lid is held on by a very large number of bolts. This is the part that eats your afternoon. It is not technically difficult — anyone who can run a socket can do it — but it is tedious and time-consuming simply because there are so many fasteners standing between you and the modules inside. Budget patience rather than skill.

This is the moment the whole job succeeds or fails. In the documented swap, the shop used a hydraulic scissor lift to lower the pack down from the truck. That equipment exists precisely because removing an EV battery demands professional-grade gear rated for the weight. A scissor lift gives you a stable, controlled descent for 1,800 pounds. A guess and a prayer gives you a wrecked pack and possibly a wrecked technician.

One point worth noting for owners cross-shopping repair difficulty across Ford's EV lineup: the Lightning is comparatively friendly here. The Mach-E crossover requires adaptors mounted to its lifting points just to create enough clearance to drop the battery out. That adaptor requirement makes the Mach-E more susceptible to damage during removal, and that battery mounting design is poorly thought out for exactly that reason. The truck asks less of you.

Here is the counterintuitive lesson from this particular repair. Only one of the battery modules had actually failed, not the entire pack. The Lightning's extended-range battery is built from nine modules — eight 56-cell modules and one 40-cell module. The standard-range pack also uses nine modules, all of them 40-cell units, and both packs occupy the same physical space despite the different cell counts inside.

You might assume that with eight healthy modules and one bad one, the fix is to pull the single dead module and drop in a good one. It is not. Hunting down one unhealthy module and swapping only that is far harder than replacing the entire pack as a unit. So the whole 1,800-pound assembly comes out, even though the vast majority of it was working fine. That is the economics of EV repair as it stands, and it is the reason a single failed cell group can total an otherwise healthy battery.

The replacement pack does not always arrive perfect. In this swap, one of the connectors on the new battery was broken, along with its coolant hose. The fix was to move the good connectors and coolant hose from the old battery onto the replacement — a reminder that even a "new" part can need parts of the old one to be serviceable. Inspect what you receive before you bolt it in.
