## The Story

A drive in the electric Boxster reveals Porsche has made an impressive electric sports car.

You may recall a decade or so ago, one of the hottest conversation topics in the food industry was the then-new Impossible Burger. Its aim – like its Beyond Burger rival – was to convince carnivorous foodies to swap beef for plant-based patties so close in taste and character to the real thing that they would brown in cooking and ‘bleed’ when rare.

Could this be the Impossible Sports Car?. The new Porsche 718 Boxster – to be officially revealed in early 2027 – is a purely battery-powered successor to the Boxster we know and love. As it enters its final development stage, we’re joining prototype testing in Mallorca, in the passenger seat on the road and behind the wheel on track. It’s a single-motor, rear-wheel-drive car and we’re about to discover it has its own equivalents to faux blood and real-meat texture: a facsimile of a flat-six engine noise, artificial gearchanges and a handling balance as close as possible to the previous petrol Boxster’s.

Our first glimpse of the new, 983-generation Boxster is underground, in a private car park in Palma. There’s a bevy of Boxster prototypes here, kept away from prying eyes. Our driver will be Frank Moser, vice president for the 718 and 911 model lines at Porsche. This car no doubt occupies rather a lot of his time and his thoughts.

Graphic decals and black paint disguise the new car’s lines, and the Porsche badges are blanked off, but it’s instantly recognisable as a Boxster. Overall the shape is similar to its predecessor, with sharper, more modern details. Moser isn’t disclosing any numbers relating to the 983 just yet, including dimensions, but it is ‘a little bit wider’ than the old 982, with a longer wheelbase.

So far, prototypes have covered nearly 2.5 million miles of testing in more than 20 countries. The hardware is finalised and the remaining work is mostly on software. Final fine-tuning touches will likely be signed off following ice testing in Sweden in the new year.

As we climb aboard, another prototype drives past. It emits not a whir but a sound very much like a traditional Porsche flat-six, light ‘exhaust’ crackles on the overrun and all. Interesting. It’s quite a drop into the low-set passenger seat, just like in the 982. In fact, it feels very much like we’re sitting in the old car. Some of the interior is covered by shrouds but the seating position and general architecture feel very similar, with decent space for two tall-ish occupants.

Immediately behind the seats there’s a slight bulge in the bulkhead. Moser explains that’s where the 800V battery is positioned. It’s roughly where the engine was in the ICE Boxster, for a traditional mid-engined balance – weight distribution is 40:60 front-to-rear – and that low seating position. Porsche calls the layout E-Core, with the battery a partially stressed chassis component. The 983 is the company’s stiffest ever convertible road car.

The platform is a redevelopment of the previous 718’s, now sharing more components with the 911. The front structure is similar to the old car’s, with MacPherson strut suspension, while the rear is all new, using elements from the current 911 Carrera GTS, including its rear-wheel-steering system – a first for the Boxster. The power steering is also from the 911 GTS, while the e-motor is related to the Macan’s.

We get underway in comparative silence, barring the natural whir of the motor and muted tyre noise. The 983 can be driven with this natural accompaniment or with selectable propulsion sounds. The first is the sci-fi-esque E‑Sound – toggled by a switch on the dash where you’d find the sport exhaust button in older Porsches – with two settings: Sport, or bassier Sport Plus.

Moser mentions finalising the sound was a long process, blending elements from the Taycan and electric Macan. The party piece, however, is the separate E‑Shift mode, with artificial gearchanges as recently applied in the Taycan E-Shift. When it’s selected, what sounds an awful lot like a petrol flat-six comes to life. The sound is based on that of the 4-litre naturally aspirated engine in the previous 718 GTS. There are speakers front and rear, with around 80 per cent of the sound from the rear speaker outside the car, for an experience as similar as possible to a conventional mid-engined car.

It’s the product of countless recordings of the 4-litre in all manner of other scenarios, but it’s not an audio playback; the sound is generated in real time, linked to the load upon the motor and the level of acceleration, deceleration and regenerative braking. It’s infinitely more realistic than the similar tool in Hyundai’s Ioniq 5 N and 6 N models, though it still has an inescapably synthetic character.

It reminds me a little of the sound you’d hear in a high-level computer game or simulator, but it does correspond exactly to what the car is doing, and feels very natural as a result. The sound changes with the driving modes too, emitting more of an ‘exhaust’ burble in Sport mode, as the 4-litre did in real life.


## Why It Matters

It feels like there’s more ultimate grip than evo’s old Boxster GTS 4.0 long-term test car (the 983’s newly developed Pirelli P Zero R tyres are wider than its predecessor’s), both laterally and longitudinally, however it still has very similar handling characteristics.


## CarMotion Daily's Take

The electric Boxster will launch into, essentially, a market of one in 2027, though it will be joined later by Alpine’s electric A110. Watch this space over the coming weeks. Electric momentum is uneven across the industry, and moves like this reshape how buyers compare EV lineups. Range, charging speed, and price still dominate EV shopping — every new entry forces competitors to either match or reposition. Watch for EPA range certifications and DC fast-charge benchmarks once independent tests land. Pricing tiers, software updates, and any regional trim adjustments will tell us how serious the launch intent is.
