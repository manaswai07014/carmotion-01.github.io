## The Story

CarScoops gets behind the wheel of the Chinese EV, IM5 Performance, to see if the luxury claims can stand up to its 767-horsepower output.

Many of the new EVs emerging from China in recent years are SUVs. However, IM is one of those brands that believes sedans still play a crucial role in the future of the automobile industry, and we aimed to see if that's true by spending time with one of its models. The oddly shaped sedan you're looking at is the IM5 Performance, and it aims to take on the Tesla Model 3 Performance.

The IM5 Performance is rather insane, in all the best ways. Electric vehicles are routinely criticized for lacking personality, being a bore to drive, and largely failing to excite traditional automotive enthusiasts. The IM5 proved itself to be different.

Brad Anderson/CarScoops
When the IM Motors brand was launched in Australia last year, the company's official name was IM Presented by MG Motor. That wacky name appears to have been dropped, and it's now simply being marketed as IM. The IM5 is one of two models it sells locally, with the other being the IM6. Whereas the IM5 rivals the Tesla Model 3, the IM6 has been crafted as a competitor to the Model Y.

There are three versions of the IM5, starting at a competitive AU$57,990 ($39,900), including all fees, and rising to AU$64,990 ($44,700), and AU$74,990 ($51,600) for the flagship Performance that we drove. It uses a sizeable 100-kWh nickel-manganese-cobalt (NMC) battery and two electric motors with a combined 767 horsepower (572 kW) and 591 pound-feet (802 Nm) of torque. To put those figures into perspective, a Hyundai Ioniq 6 N has 650 hp (478 kW) and 546 lb-ft (740 Nm) of torque, while the Model 3 Performance 'makes do' with 510 hp (380 kW) and 546 lb-ft (740 Nm).

The IM5 Performance's claimed 0-100 kph (62 mph) time of 3.2 seconds is seriously rapid and matches the Ioniq 6 N, though is slightly slower than the Model 3 Performance's 3.1 seconds. This in partly because the IM5 weighs about 400 kg (882 Nm) more, in large part due to the 100-kWh battery, whereas the Tesla has a 79-kWh pack.

Brad Anderson/CarScoops
We've driven plenty of EVs from China over the past few years from ho-hum MGS6 SUV to China's first-ever supercar, the Yangwang U9, and we've found that many of them have unimaginative interiors that look like exact replicas of what Tesla has done. The IM5 is a little different, sporting a cabin more reminiscent of a Porsche Taycan.

It includes a single, 26.3-inch display serving as both the digital gauge cluster and the infotainment display, and includes wireless Apple CarPlay and Android Auto as standard. Then, like a Porsche Taycan, the IM5 has a portrait-oriented 10.5-inch second screen that's used to control most vehicle functions. It's surprising that more modern cars don't have a screen like this, as it's easier to reach and control while on the move than the Tesla-style screens positioned further up on the dashboard.

Brad Anderson/CarScoops
Elsewhere, the IM5 Performance's cabin is clad in soft-touch leather on the dashboard, door panels, center console, and steering wheel, giving it an upmarket and premium feel. It also comes standard with a wireless and ventilated phone charger and a pair of cup holders. The door pockets are small, as is the center console, so storage is a bit of an issue.

Like the front, the rear of the IM5 is a lovely place to spend time, and it has more headroom than a Model 3. Perhaps the biggest annoyance is that it includes a huge panoramic glass roof with no sunshade, just like a Tesla. The saving grace for the IM5 is that it's available with an optional AU$109 ($75) sunshade, though it's not retractable. Rear visibility is also extraordinarily poor as the window is absolutely tiny. The IM5 also has the smallest rear view mirror I've ever seen. If there was ever a car that needed a digital rear view mirror, it would be this one, but alas, it doesn't have one.


## Why It Matters

At slow speeds, the rear wheels turn in the opposite direction to the fronts, making the IM5 exceptionally easy to maneuver through tight car parks.
