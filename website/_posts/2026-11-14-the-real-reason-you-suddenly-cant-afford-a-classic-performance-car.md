## The Story

The modern classic market has gone into hyperdrive, and Meaden’s not impressed.

Even if you pay only scant attention to auction results, it can’t have escaped your notice that the classic and particularly modern classic supercar market went nuts over the summer. Most noticeably in the US, where the auctioneers’ gavels fell on record bid after record bid.

Even if you accept that the US is a world of its own so far as spending power goes, these prices make no sense. Just a couple of months ago, in preparation for our ’90s Supercar Eras test, I insured an extremely nice, low-mileage 550M for £130k, which was about the top of the market. I’m not sure what they were smoking in the auction rooms at Monterey, but there’s no way a 12k-mile 550M is now worth three times that. Manual 360 Modenas are going the same way, even though they’re far from a great Ferrari.

Why am I bothered?. Aside from feeling uncharacteristic socialist pangs about the increasingly obscene, world-skewing gulf between the mega-wealthy and everyone else, as someone who has enjoyed a life-long love affair with cars, it’s how this wealth gap has slowly corrupted the classic and modern classic car scene that I find increasingly unsavoury. It seems our world is now divided between true car enthusiasts, who were spending their money on cars long before they became asset-class items, and the wealthy enthusiasts who happen to have found investment vehicles (excuse the pun) that rarely if ever get driven.

There aren’t many advantages to being 55 years old, but it does give you some perspective. I’m old enough to remember when the people who bought big-ticket supercars did so knowing they were going to take a hammering. Both in running costs and depreciation. Even McLaren F1s lost significant value. Rather heroically, those people bought supercars because they really wanted to, not because they were forced to buy cars they didn’t want so they might be considered for an allocation of the one they did, nor because it was somewhere to park money with a view to making more money without paying any tax.

Buying a performance car shouldn’t feel like speculating on the stock market, and it certainly shouldn’t feel like clawing your way onto the property ladder. Sadly, this appears to be where we’re at. Great if you’re on it. Crap if you’re not.
