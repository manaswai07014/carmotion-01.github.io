## The Story

*TopGear* reports that Freshly minted: is the Codalunga Speedster *peak* Pagani? — here's the gist of what's worth knowing.

The Zonda is the boss, the Huayra a rush and the Utopia pure art. But could this Coda be the ultimate?. Published: 11 Nov 2026 External link to Top Gear Magazine Subscription – 3 months for £6On my desk as I write this, weighing down an untidy pile of receipts to expense and envelopes I’ll put off opening, is a little sculpture of a Pagani Huayra Codalunga Speedster. It glints expensively but weighs almost nothing, because it’s milled from the same aircraft grade aluminium Pagani deems worthy of its real cars.

Each new Utopia is home to over 700 CNC-machined parts. The slender steering wheel starts life as a 40kg cylinder like a wheel of cheese. The delicate throttle pedal is withered away from a block bigger than your TV remote. And the swarf isn’t melted down for the next batch of bits. It’s sent off to industries who obsess less over quality. Even little keepsakes like an exquisite paperweight don’t use offcuts. That’s not the Pagani way.

I only mention that miniature memento because like the scarf received by the little boy in The Snowman, it’s tangible evidence this day ever happened. A hazy dream on one of Italy’s best kept secret roads, in what should be the final branch of the Pagani Huayra family tree. If the factory can bring itself to stop.

It’s the day after we shot the three Pagani Roadsters you recently read about. Jethro’s flown off to drive his thirty-seventh new hypercar of the working week, and I’ve stayed behind with photographer Mark and video team Harry and Mitch to meet one more Modenese aristocrat.

It’s a late arrival from a VIP event on the other side of Italy where it’s been holding court with Pagani’s most fanatical customers. The 15 year old Huayra has now arrived in the Pagani retirement phase. It’s been replaced, we’ve said our farewells. But Pagani just can’t resist another special. And another. Go on then, just one more. A coda. A stretched one. The long goodbye.

It’s 6.30 in the morning as our chocker hire car rounds a bend on the Strada Statale 17 to reveal the valley where a 1:1 scale Huayra Codalunga Speedster has recently vacated its transporter. We’re chattering away making a shoot wishlist but the car falls silent as this shimmering pistachio speck draws nearer in the windscreen, an art-deco spaceship sent from the future by a race of steampunk superbeings.

As decreed in the ancient supercar scrolls, all car names sound better in Italian, and this last hurrah (yeah, right!) Huayra agrees. It’s the longtail, inspired by the flowing aerodynamic flourishes of late 1960s and early ’70s racing cars. Horacio Pagani himself has recently added two restored Porsche 917s to his personal collection. He adores this era of primitive aero and stunning profiles. But in Italian, you don’t mutter ‘long tail’. You say ‘Codalunga’, with a flourish.

It’s a product of what Pagani calls, and I promise I’m not joking, Grandi Complicazioni. That translates as ‘major complications’, and it’s the sector deep inside Pagani that deals with the bespoke, the rarefied, and the demands other Pagani owners dare not ask for. In this case, a Huayra that doesn’t share a single panel with the standard car.

Grandi Complicazioni has previously dreamt up unicorn limited editions like the roofless Zonda HP Barchetta (only three built), the Huayra’s evil track-focused cousin Imola (six of those, plus eight roadsters) and five Huayra Codalunga coupes complete with an 828bhp version of the 6.0-litre twin turbo V12.

The Codalunga Speedster is actually common as muck for Grandi Complicazioni. Minty fresh here is the factory prototype but they’ve sold 10 customer cars. Depreciation’s gotta sting, eh?. Should you be among the 10 and beset by owners of hard-top Codalungas gloating their car is rarer, you can retort that your Speedster is more powerful. The version with the removeable one-piece roof has 852bhp. Twenty four more. And is therefore better. Welcome to the joint most powerful road-going Pagani yet.

But you needn’t worry about the roofless Coda having the structural rigidity of a politician’s spine. The Speedster’s super strong carbon fibre and titanium spliced monocoque chassis is unique. The windscreen is severely chopped too. Those are expensive procedures if you’re making a thousand cars, never mind 10, and partly explains why each Speedster costs €11 million. While the camera botherers swarm it with suction cups, tripods and mounts, I tell myself I’m taking my time to absorb the details.


## Why It Matters

I’ve driven Bugattis with twice the power of this, I’ve driven a Rimac with almost three times the power, but the **Pagani** is somehow even more shockingly savage, maybe because you don’t expect art to be capable of such raw brutality.
