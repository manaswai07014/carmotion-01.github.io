## The Story

BMW i3 name is back, but now in a much more conventional form than before. It&#x27;s quick, efficient and retains Munich&#x27;s trademark dynamic character. Not much comes close.

RRPfrom £53,425Superb refinement, performance, handling and rangeHMI is a challenge initially, seatsBMW’s commitment to electrification is epitomised by the fact that the next M3 will be launched first as an all-electric model with the ICE version coming later. Before then, we get BMW’s take on its first regular electric 3-series, in this instance the i3 50 xDrive, the current range-topper. I drove it after the M350 xDrive so I’d overcome the shock of the Neue Klasse interior with its weird, squarish steering wheel and radical Panoramic iDrive instrument display, both of which work well, surprisingly well in the case of the wheel.

BMW is confident in its product with this new car, and that becomes clear before you’ve even stepped inside it. At the UK launch BMW parked it right next to the iconic E21 original from the ‘70s, a bold move to make given its compact dimensions and universally adored design, but it works. It looks a little tall from some angles and soft from the rear, but the sharp, horizontal graphic and dynamic bonnet creases mean this new car holds its own, and that extends to beyond the way it looks.

Externally, the ICE and BEV versions of the new 3-series appear remarkably similar, though they are on different platforms. The wheelbase of the i3 is longer, its roof height is 25mm higher, a small amount given that the battery pack is in the floor, an integral part of the structure, which improves rigidity and saves weight. Even so, the circa 2200kg i3 50 is a substantial 345kg heavier than the six-cylinder M350. For reference, that’s a match for the previous-generation electric BMW i4, and around 250kg more than the dual-motor Tesla Model 3 Performance.

There will be a sportier i3 ‘60’ with more power and the dynamic features of the M350, but the i3 50 xDrive proved quick and dynamic. It costs from £54,425 and offers an M350-beating 469bhp and 476lb ft of electric power, and a range of between 450 and 567 miles. The 108.7 kWh battery pack charges from zero to 100 per cent in 11 hours at 11kW, says BMW, but of course most UK homes are single-phase 7.6kW. At an ultra-fast, 400kW rapid charger, you can go from 10 to 80 per cent charge in 21mins.

The throttle response is well calibrated too, with progressive response, which can be sharpened with the drive modes. Floor the throttle and it feels properly quick, quicker than the M350 all-out, though it’s not as quick in the benchmark sprint (0-62 takes 4.7sec) and it’s not going to be hassling the ICE versions on the autobahn because it’s limited to 124mph. You can call up an ‘engine sound’ but to me it sounds like an electrified train working hard to pick up speed with a slightly ethereal quality – after a short while it gets annoying. Happily, the sound of silence is pleasingly relaxing, particularly how the i3 rolls quietly over many surfaces.

It’s xDrive, but drive is sent exclusively to the rear until the grip runs out. Like the M350, the i3 has a lovely chassis balance, with a willingness to turn and a natural feel though it lacks adaptive dampers and is shod with less sporty Bridgestone Turanzas. At one point on a challenging road we had a Hyundai i20 N filling the rearview mirror but the i3 didn’t hold it up in the corners, even the trickier sections, and when there was a straight, the breath-taking full-throttle acceleration gapped it impressively. Shame the seats are neither as comfortable or supportive as the M350’s.

Pull back on the stubby gear selector and you get strong regen braking allowing one-pedal driving on twisting roads. You can choose the level of regen in one of the menus or select ‘Adaptive’ which, with a route set in the nav, is remarkable. It ‘knows’ the road and will use regen to slow the car to the right degree for each corner. The oddest thing is approaching blind corners and knowing from the level of regen how tight it will be. A great mode for roads you don’t know, especially in difficult conditions. It’ll read the traffic in front of you to seamlessly increase regen accordingly, too, which makes stop start traffic less taxing than it would be otherwise.

Stump up some additional cash for the endless optional software packages on the i3 and you can unlock Motorway Assistant (£925 on the 50 xDrive), one of the first systems approved for hands-off motorway driving in the UK. We have little urge to let the car do the work for us, but observing just how polished it is boggles the mind – steering inputs are smooth and calculated, and it’ll confidently make lane changes itself once it recognises that you’ve checked the appropriate mirror. A speed cap of 70mph will make its use case limited for those keen to match the flow of traffic, but the execution proves just how sophisticated this package is.

The ‘Heart of Joy’ centralised control unit is the key ingredient here, developed entirely in-house from scratch when BMW couldn’t find an OEM willing to accept the challenge. It will come into its own in full-blooded performance applications like the incoming M3, but even in the i3, the instantaneous, frictionless communication between components give it a level of sophistication, both dynamically and in terms of everyday useability, that nothing on the market does. Everything from its imperceptible ‘soft-stop’ feature to the response of the central infotainment make it clear that this car is truly next generation.

First seen in the iX3 SUV, the aforementioned Neue Klasse steering wheel certainly takes some time to get used to. It’s an awkward design with no clear top and bottom, unusual transparent touch pads on either side and capacitive buttons back lit only when the car feels you need to access that control – it looks odd, but in reality the system works well, with accidental button activation (as seen in the Mk8 Volkswagen Golf) yet to be experienced. The rest of the cabin gets a clean, horizontal look with minimal buttons everywhere except the door cards, where you get physical switches for the likes of window controls, mirrors and seats.


## Why It Matters

There are a lot of settings to play around with, which can become a little overwhelming, but Amazon Alexa Plus integration means you can often bypass it all by asking the car to make the change for you.
