I thought living in Detroit would be relatively safe in the coming climate crisis thanks to our fresh water and temperate climate. No real natural disasters routinely threaten this pleasant peninsula besides the slow-moving one of the freeze-thaw cycle every winter. Then, the night before I drove a 2027 Ford Maverick Lobo 300 miles on a mini-road trip to retrieve oil wrestling supplies in Ohio, my hometown was hit with its first tornado in 29 years. Suddenly, I was huddled with my three dogs in my basement, watching the lights flicker. I thought of Gary, Indiana. I thought of Flint. I thought of the U.S.'s de-funding of FEMA and rollback of regulations and rejection of anything that might save us, and then I thought, absolutely nothing is stopping me from using that Maverick Lobo to pick up 16 gallons of mineral oil and 300 pounds of bentonite now.

Pickup ownership always seemed like a drag to me. They aren't particularly fun to drive, and if you're the average truck owner your bed remains empty most of the time until, inevitably, your days off start getting booked up by friends moving house. And I will admit to seeing one too many selfish truck drivers in my time. I started associating the entire vehicle type with self-centered behavior.

That was until I heard the call for community building, with this little truck as the tool. Any car can be what you make of it. I learned that owning a truck can be an act of responsibility to the people around you, one that supplies joy rather than tiresome stress.

Full disclosure: Ford loaned me a Maverick Lobo with a full tank of gas to do with as I pleased for a week. Erin Marquis/Jalopnik
To be fair, the Ford Maverick Lobo does not scream "reliable citizen," especially when it's as low to the ground and blacked-out as this particular truck. And with a starting price of $41,425, and my tester topping out at $44,175, it's not exactly what I'd call a truck of the people. An unadorned base Maverick, however, squeaks in under $30,000, which these days is practically a steal.

The Lobo is Ford's "street truck" version of its money-printing little unibody ute, but it comes with the same turbocharged 2.0-liter inline-4 EcoBoost engine as the base XT. Gas mileage was pretty good on my road trip (I scored about 28.1 mpg), but it's clear the "street" part of this street truck is more about the looks than packing in extra power. It's a little lower, the suspension is a little stiffer, the handling is a little sportier, and the 7-speed automatic shifts a little quicker over the base Maverick. It remains as easy to drive as a crossover, artfully maneuvering into city parking spots and in and out of traffic, but handles a little more like a sports car, all while having a useful bed on the back. Truly, it's a vehicle for all seasons. Mine also came with trailer assist, which, lol. I'm sure someone is hauling something with their Lobo, but I can't imagine it.

Seven hours of solo driving in total, for folks I barely knew at the time. So, why did I do it?. Well, I didn't need to know them. A friend of a friend reposted a call for anyone with the ability to pick up the oil for the event (and the mud for the following week), and I just happened to have the ability. Plus, I knew that the folks organizing the event were trying to create space in my community among people who needed it with nothing more than a pair of blow-up pools, a sign-in sheet and mineral oil. Despite the city's resurgence, Detroit folks still don't have a lot, and queer folks have even less. As a member of both communities, I happened to have access to a vehicle like a truck and could offer it to good people in need. If you can, do.

Erin Marquis/Jalopnik
Driving across this particular corner of the Great Lakes is not interesting. It's even worse when constant storms turn the entire world outside your windows into sheets of gray, as close to a sensory deprivation tank as I want to let my chatty brain experience. Thankfully, I've been bitten by the "Dungeon Crawler Carl" bug, so I had hours of audiobook to work through and a very comfortable cabin to do it in. Ford interiors are pretty drab, but goddamn it, they are functional. This one came with lovely two-tone blue-and-lime contrast stitching in the dark gray cloth seats. The touchscreen was responsive, and things like climate controls are well laid out. I didn't find myself searching for buttons on a screen or stabbing the damn thing with my finger like a prosecutor dealing with a hostile witness.

Despite having a stiffer suspension, the Lobo's road noise was kept to a minimum, even in the rain. Its ride quality was surprisingly good, too. I kept waiting to fight with it during storm gusts, but the truck stayed firmly in its lane. The Maverick is easy to get into, easy to sit in for hours, and easy to get out of again. Also, Ford makes a hell of a heated seat. I want a seat that cooks the middle-aged mid-back pain out of me, and Ford delivers. The center console is also very usable, with several little cubbies for stashing keys, snacks, water bottles, and what-have-yous.

I was dismayed, however, to find out this very expensive truck did not have a wireless charging pad in it. For a $44,175 vehicle, that pretty simple piece of tech should be standard, in my opinion. There also seemed to be a timing problem with the windshield wipers, and they'd catch every so often on the edge of the windshield. Kind of a disappointing fault in a car with only around 24,000 miles on it. I also didn't love the weird, super thick door handles with their six bare hex bolts, but the Maverick's doors are oddly heavy, which made those thick handles worthwhile.

As for the 16 gallons of mineral oil and the 300 pounds of bentonite, they all stayed snug and secure in the bed, even in the pouring rain, thanks to the tonneau cover and bed tie-downs. Thank goodness, because I don't know how I would explain 300 pounds of bentonite melted into the back of a press truck to the fine people at Ford.
